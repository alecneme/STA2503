\subsection{Brownian motion and scaled random walks}
Let $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\{\mathcal F_t\}_{t\ge 0},\PP)$ be a filtered probability space.
A process $W=\{W_t\}_{t\ge0}$ is a \emph{standard Brownian motion} if
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $W_0=0$ and $W$ is adapted to $\{\mathcal F_t\}$;
  \item for $0\le s\le t$, $W_t-W_s\sim\N(0,\,t-s)$;
  \item $W_t-W_s$ is independent of $\mathcal F_s$;
  \item $t\mapsto W_t(\omega)$ is continuous for every $\omega\in\Omega$.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Simulating BM.} On the grid $t_k=k\Delta t$, $\Delta t=1/N_1$, we use
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bm_scheme}
W_{t_{k+1}} = W_{t_k} + \sqrt{\Delta t}\,\xi_{k+1},\qquad \xi_k \overset{iid}{\sim}\N(0,1),\qquad W_0=0,
\end{equation}
which is exact at the grid points by property 2. We take $N_1=10{,}000$.

\noindent\textbf{Scaled random walk.} Let $Z_i\overset{iid}{\sim}\pm1$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:srw}
W^{(N)}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{i=1}^{Nt} Z_i,\qquad t\in\{0,\tfrac1N,\dots,1\}.
\end{equation}
Each increment of $W^{(N)}$ has mean $0$ and variance $1/N=\Delta t$, matching those of BM.
We simulate $N_2\in\{10,50,200,1000\}$. The $1/\sqrt N$ scaling is used because it is the only scaling that keeps
$\operatorname{Var}(W^{(N)}_1)=1$ as $N\to\infty$.

\noindent\textbf{Implementation.} \texttt{simulate\_bm} draws all increments $\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\xi$
at once as an array with one row per path and one column per time step, and builds the paths
with a cumulative sum along each row, as in \eqref{eq:bm_scheme}. \texttt{scaled\_random\_walk}
does the same with $\pm1$ steps drawn by \texttt{rng.choice} and then rescales by $1/\sqrt N$,
as in \eqref{eq:srw}. A column of zeros is prepended so that every path starts at $0$.

\subsection{Market data and parameters}
The inputs below are shared by the three pricing methods of Sections~\ref{sec:crr}--\ref{sec:bs}.

\noindent\textbf{Stock price, strike and maturity.} $S_0=\$228.38$ is the NVDA closing price on
30 September 2026 (Yahoo Finance). As required by the project, the option is at-the-money,
$K=S_0$, with maturity $T=1$ year.

\noindent\textbf{Volatility.} Under GBM, daily log returns
$R_i=\ln(S_{t_i}/S_{t_{i-1}})$ are i.i.d.\ $\N\bigl((\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2)\Delta t,\ \sigma^2\Delta t\bigr)$
with $\Delta t=1/252$ (trading days per year). We therefore estimate $\sigma$ by the
annualised sample standard deviation of daily log returns (historical volatility),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_hat}
\hat\sigma=\sqrt{252}\;\operatorname{sd}(R_1,\dots,R_n),
\end{equation}
using the $n=251$ daily returns from the 252 adjusted closing prices between 30 September 2025
and 30 September 2026. The one-year window matches the maturity of the option, and adjusted
prices remove artificial jumps due to stock splits and dividends. This gives
$\hat\sigma=37.60\%$, with approximate standard error
$\hat\sigma/\sqrt{2n}\approx1.7$ percentage points. In the code, \texttt{estimate\_volatility}
computes the log returns with \texttt{np.diff(np.log(closes))} and applies \eqref{eq:sigma_hat}.

\noindent\textbf{Interest rate.} We use $r=4\%$, a round value within the range of the 1-year
US Treasury yield over the past year (approximately 3.4\%--4.6\%) and somewhat below its level
on the pricing date (4.54\% on 30 September 2026; U.S.\ Department of the Treasury, daily par
yield curve). The rate is assumed constant and continuously compounded.

\subsection{The CRR binomial model}\label{sec:crr}
With $N$ steps over $[0,T]$ and $\Delta t = T/N$, the stock moves from $S$ to $uS$ or $dS$, where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:crr}
u=e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}},\qquad d=\frac1u,\qquad \tilde p=\frac{e^{r\Delta t}-d}{u-d}.
\end{equation}
No arbitrage requires $d<e^{r\Delta t}<u$, which holds for our parameters.
Note that $e^{r\Delta t}$ is the continuously-compounded version of the $1+r$
rate used in lecture. Similarly, for the model we assume a finite and constant r.
After $n$ steps with $j$ up-moves the price is $S_n(j)=S_0\,u^{j}d^{\,n-j}$, $j=0,\dots,n$.
Since $d=1/u$ the tree recombines, so only the number of up-moves matters, not their order
(e.g.\ $S_2(HT)=S_2(TH)=S_2(1)$ in the path notation of the lectures).
A European call is priced by backward induction:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:backward}
V_N(j)=\bigl(S_N(j)-K\bigr)^+,\qquad
V_n(j)=e^{-r\Delta t}\Bigl[\tilde p\,V_{n+1}(j+1)+(1-\tilde p)\,V_{n+1}(j)\Bigr],
\end{equation}
and the price is $V_0(0)$.
An example tree for NVDA is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tree} (Section~\ref{sec:binomial}).

\noindent\textbf{Number of steps.} We use $N=100$: at this size the price is within about 0.2\%
of the Black--Scholes value and, since the error decreases roughly like $1/N$
(Section~\ref{sec:binomial}), further steps add little. To study convergence we also price the
option for every $N$ from 1 to 300.

\noindent\textbf{Implementation.} \texttt{crr\_parameters} computes $u$, $d$, $\tilde p$ from
\eqref{eq:crr} and raises an error if the no-arbitrage condition $d<e^{r\Delta t}<u$ fails.
\texttt{prices\_at\_step} returns all $n+1$ prices $S_n(j)$ at once by treating $j$ as an array.
\texttt{backward\_induction} implements \eqref{eq:backward} with array slicing: for the vector
of values $V_{n+1}$, the up-children of all nodes are \texttt{V[1:]} and the down-children are
\texttt{V[:-1]}, so each step back is a single vectorised line. \texttt{binomial\_call} chains
these functions. Sample paths through the tree (\texttt{simulate\_tree\_paths}) draw up-moves
with probability $\tilde p$ by comparing uniform random numbers with $\tilde p$, map them to
factors $u$ or $d$ with \texttt{np.where}, and take a cumulative product along each path.

\subsection{Geometric Brownian motion and Monte Carlo}\label{sec:gbm}
Under GBM, $dS_t=\mu S_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW_t$. Applying It\^o's formula to $\log S_t$,
\[
d\log S_t=\Bigl(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2\Bigr)dt+\sigma\,dW_t
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
S_t=S_0\exp\Bigl[\Bigl(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2\Bigr)t+\sigma W_t\Bigr].
\]

For pricing we work under the risk-neutral measure $\Q$, where $\mu$ is replaced by $r$.
We also use $\mu=r$ for the simulated sample paths, so that both models are simulated under the
same measure (the binomial paths use $\tilde p$); estimating NVDA's real-world drift is not required.
The Euler--Maruyama scheme on the grid $t_k=k\Delta t$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:euler}
S_{t_{k+1}} = S_{t_k}\bigl(1+r\Delta t+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\xi_{k+1}\bigr),\qquad \xi_k\overset{iid}{\sim}\N(0,1).
\end{equation}
With $M$ simulated paths, the Monte Carlo price and its standard error are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mc}
\widehat C = e^{-rT}\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^M \bigl(S_T^{(m)}-K\bigr)^+,\qquad
\mathrm{SE}=\frac{s}{\sqrt M},
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the sample standard deviation of the discounted payoffs; a 95\% confidence interval is $\widehat C\pm1.96\,\mathrm{SE}$.

\noindent\textbf{Numerical parameters.} The Euler--Maruyama scheme uses the same time grid as
the tree ($N=100$, $\Delta t=0.01$), so both models are compared on the same discretisation.
We use $M=100{,}000$ paths, which gives a standard error of about 0.5\% of the option price;
since the error decreases only like $1/\sqrt M$, halving it would require four times as many paths.

\noindent\textbf{Implementation.} \texttt{simulate\_gbm\_euler} implements \eqref{eq:euler}
with the same structure as the tree simulation: it draws all standard normals $\xi$ at once,
forms the one-step factors $1+r\Delta t+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\xi$, and takes a cumulative
product along each path. \texttt{mc\_call\_price} computes the estimator and standard error
\eqref{eq:mc} from the terminal prices, using the sample standard deviation (with $M-1$ in the
denominator).

\subsection{Black--Scholes benchmark}\label{sec:bs}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bs}
C_{BS}=S_0\,\Phi(d_+)-Ke^{-rT}\Phi(d_-),\qquad
d_\pm=\frac{\ln(S_0/K)+(r\pm\tfrac12\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T},
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF, evaluated with \texttt{scipy.stats.norm.cdf} in
\texttt{black\_scholes\_call}.

\subsection{Verification and reproducibility}
\noindent\textbf{Verification.} Each function was tested against a known answer before use:
the backward induction reproduces the 3-period example from the lectures ($V_0=2.048$); the
no-arbitrage check correctly rejects inconsistent inputs; for both the tree and the Euler
scheme, the simulated mean of $S_T$ under $\Q$ matches $S_0e^{rT}$ within Monte Carlo error,
as required by the martingale property of discounted prices; and the Monte Carlo confidence
interval was checked for coverage over repeated runs (Section~\ref{sec:comparison}).

\noindent\textbf{Reproducibility.} All randomness comes from NumPy random generators with fixed
seeds (\texttt{default\_rng(2503)} in each notebook, and separate fixed seeds for the tests), so
re-running a notebook from top to bottom reproduces every figure and number in this report.
Market data are downloaded once and stored as CSV files, so results do not change when new
data become available. Every figure is produced by the code and saved directly into the report,
and all reported numbers come from a single top-to-bottom run of each notebook. The code is

  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/part1_rw_N10.pdf}
  \caption{Scaled random walk, $N_2=10$.}
\end{subfigure}
\caption{Five sample paths of each process on $[0,1]$. \label{fig:bm_rw10}}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:bm_rw10} compares Brownian motion simulated with $N_1 = 10{,}000$ steps to a
scaled random walk with only $N_2 = 10$ steps. The random walk is piecewise linear: each path
changes direction only at the grid points $t = 0.1, 0.2, \dots, 1$ and moves in straight lines
in between, with every step of size $\pm 1/\sqrt{10} \approx \pm 0.32$. The Brownian motion
paths, on the other hand, oscillate at every time scale and show no visible straight segments.
Even so, the two processes already share some features: both start at $0$, have mean zero, and
spread out by a similar amount by $t = 1$, since the scaling gives
$\operatorname{Var}(W^{(N_2)}_1) = 1$, the same as $\operatorname{Var}(W_1)$.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/part1_rw_vs_bm.pdf}
\caption{Scaled random walks (colour) for increasing $N_2$, with the Brownian motion paths of
Figure~\ref{fig:bm_rw10}(a) in grey. \label{fig:rw_vs_bm}}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:rw_vs_bm} shows what happens as $N_2$ increases. When $N_2 = 200$, the scaled
random walk starts looking more like Brownian motion, and for $N_2 = 1000$ the two are
indistinguishable in character, even though the coloured and grey paths are independent
realizations. This happens because Brownian motion is the limit in distribution of the scaled
random walk (Donsker's theorem). As we increase $N_2$, the walk changes value more frequently
(every $1/N_2$ units of time), and at the same time each step shrinks to $\pm 1/\sqrt{N_2}$.
The scaling is done because without it, $\operatorname{Var}(W_1)$ would equal $N_2$ and the
walk would blow up, whereas with it $\operatorname{Var}(W^{(N_2)}_1) = 1$ for every $N_2$.
Each straight segment has slope $\pm\sqrt{N_2}$, which becomes steeper as $N_2$ grows; this is
the discrete counterpart of the fact that Brownian motion paths are nowhere differentiable.

Figure~\ref{fig:tree} shows the CRR tree for NVDA with $N=4$, for which $u=1.1618$,
$d=0.8607$ and $\tilde p=0.4959$. Because $d=1/u$, an up-move followed by a down-move returns
the stock to its starting value, so the tree recombines: \$228.38 reappears at $t=0.5$ and
$t=1$, and after $n$ steps there are only $n+1$ distinct prices rather than $2^n$. The moves
are proportional rather than additive, so in dollar terms an up-move is larger than a down-move
(\$228.38 rises to \$265.34 or falls to \$196.57 in the first step). Finally, since $N$ is even,
one terminal node lies exactly at the strike $K=S_0=\$228.38$.



With $N=100$ steps, the CRR price of the one-year at-the-money call is \$31.3425.
Figure~\ref{fig:convergence} shows that as $N$ increases the binomial price converges to the
Black--Scholes price of \$31.4097: the error is $-\$0.66$ for $N=10$, $-\$0.07$ for $N=100$
and $-\$0.007$ for $N=1000$. The product $N\times(\text{error})$ stays close to $-6.7$ for all
$N$ we tried, so the error decreases roughly like $1/N$. The convergence is not monotone: the
price oscillates between even $N$, which lie below Black--Scholes, and odd $N$, which lie above
it (e.g.\ \$31.4731 for $N=101$). This is due to the kink of the payoff $(S_T-K)^+$ at the
strike: for even $N$ the middle terminal node sits exactly at $K$ (as in Figure~\ref{fig:tree}),
where the payoff is zero, whereas for odd $N$ the two closest nodes straddle $K$. As $N$ grows,
$\tilde p$ also approaches $\tfrac12$ ($0.4959$ for $N=4$, $0.4992$ for $N=100$), and the
tree increasingly resembles the symmetric random walk of Part~I, whose limit is the
continuous-time model behind Black--Scholes.
